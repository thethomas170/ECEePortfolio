Project · Fall 2024 – Fall 2025

Banks County Community Tech Outreach

A hobby that turned into a service project. I built and configured three computers for members of my community, taught the people receiving them how to use and maintain the machines, and provided ongoing laptop help to elderly neighbors who had nobody else to ask.

  • Hardware assembly
  • Thermal & airflow planning
  • Cable management
  • Power supply sizing
  • BIOS & OS configuration
  • Technical teaching

How it started

I started building PCs because I wanted one and could not afford to buy a prebuilt machine at the specification I wanted. Learning to do it myself was cheaper, and it turned out to be far more interesting than the purchase would have been. Choosing parts forces you to understand why the parts exist: what the power supply has to deliver under load, why the cooler has to move a specific amount of heat, how much airflow the case actually provides, and which of those constraints will bite you first.

Once I had done it a few times, people in Banks County started asking. Some of them wanted a machine built for a specific budget. Some of them had a laptop that had become unusable and did not know whether the problem was fixable or whether they needed to spend money. Over about a year, that turned into a standing arrangement where I was the person who handled this for anyone who asked.

What the work actually involves

The part people underestimate is that a build is a systems-integration problem, not a sequence of parts going into slots. Every decision constrains another one. The cooler has to clear the memory. The radiator has to fit the case with the fans mounted in the direction you want the air moving. The power supply has to have the right connectors, not just the right wattage. The front-panel and fan headers have to be routed before the board is mounted, because afterward you cannot reach them.

Airflow is the decision I spend the most time on, because it is the one that is invisible when it is wrong. A machine that is quietly running hot will throttle, and the owner will conclude the computer is slow rather than that the intake is fighting the exhaust. Planning intake and exhaust as a deliberate path through the case, rather than mounting fans wherever there are screw holes, is most of the difference between a build that stays quiet and one that does not.

The teaching half

Handing someone a working computer is only half of the job, and it is the easier half. A machine that the owner is afraid to change is a machine that will be broken again in six months. So for each build I walked the person through what was inside it, what they could safely do themselves, what to do when it got slow, and when something was actually a problem rather than normal behavior.

The laptop help for elderly neighbors was mostly this, without the hardware. Often the fix took two minutes and the real work was explaining it in a way that left the person able to handle it themselves next time, rather than dependent on me being available. I got noticeably better at that over the year, and it is the skill from this project I expect to use most: being able to explain a technical situation to somebody who does not share your vocabulary, without being condescending about it.

Why it matters to my engineering path

This project is the reason the hardware side of electrical engineering felt familiar rather than intimidating when I got to Georgia Tech. Working inside a case teaches you that specifications are constraints that interact, that heat is a design problem rather than an afterthought, and that power delivery determines what the rest of the system can do — which turns out to be true at the scale of a desk and at the scale of a grid.

It also connects directly to my Discovery Project: the gaming PC that the screen-time alert monitors is a machine I built.
